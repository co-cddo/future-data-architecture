# Summary

Cross-government data principles are intended to provide a basis for and guide the ways in which public sector organisations _collaboratively use_ data to solve problems. Nine principles are currently proposed:

1. Manage data as a public sector asset
2. Move towards “Once Only”
3. Minimise data movement
4. Maximise machine readability
5. Use common capabilities and standards
6. Be transparent
7. Act lawfully and ethically
8. Secure data according to its value
9. Design user-centric data systems

# Introduction

## Principle objectives

Cross-government data principles are intended to provide a basis for and guide the ways in which the public sector _collaboratively uses_ data to solve problems.

Doing this better is core to the objectives of the [Blueprint for Modern Digital Government](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/a-blueprint-for-modern-digital-government/a-blueprint-for-modern-digital-government-html), and particularly its priority to:

<blockquote>
“Join up public sector services: enabling next-generation public services, better supporting businesses, redesigning cross-organisation end-to-end service delivery, ensuring services are consistently high standard, and acting as one public sector.”
</blockquote>

However, the role of data in delivering the Blueprint is often implied rather than set out explicitly. These principles are intended to bridge the gap between vision and implementation, and guide data professionals and senior decision-makers across the public sector in making decisions about data that contribute to the overall strategy.

The principles also serve as an update and replacement to the [Data Sharing Governance Framework](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/data-sharing-governance-framework/data-sharing-governance-framework) which was aligned to the now defunct [National Data Strategy](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/uk-national-data-strategy/national-data-strategy).

## Scope

The data principles aim to guide the ways in which the public sector _collaboratively uses_ data to solve problems. Collaborative use of data refers to the combination of two practices:

- **Data sharing:** making data available to others.
- **Data processing:** carrying out operations on data to extract value.

These two practices are often carried out consecutively by separate actors (e.g. Person A provides data to Person B, then Person B processes that data). However, the boundaries between data sharing and processing are sometimes less absolute (e.g. Person B submits a data processing request to Person A who owns data of interest, and receives only the results of the processing).

**These principles primarily address the data sharing component of collaborative data use**, which is often a secondary focus to processing in established data regulation and policy. They should help shape public sector data sharing initiatives that involve:

- **Data access:** providing permission for others to view and use data.
- **Data linkage**: joining data together from separate sources.
- **Data discovery**: identifying and explore data prior to full processing.
- **Data publication**: making data assets available for others to reuse.

More broadly, the principles will have implications for public sector:

- **Data management**: the overall processes of organising and maintaining data assets throughout their lifecycle.
- **Data architecture**: the blueprints for data management, describing how data can flow across a data system.

## Relationship to other principles, policies and initiatives

The principles are designed to overarch and complement, rather than supersede or repeat, other principles that guide established data and technology policy in the public sector. Exercising these principles at scale will entail changes to way organisations organise and deliver around their data assets in the future, but without excessive step-changes or contradiction to existing principles. It will also require new government policy, guidance and solution enablers to be produced where exercising data sharing principles is currently challenging.

Data sharing principles could also be considered a subset of enterprise architecture principles that provide a more general basis for decision-making across an enterprise. The data principles should embody the essence of enterprise architecture principles while offering more subject constraints and direction. ## Audience

The data principles are for strategic decision-makers working with data across the public sector, but primarily within central government. This includes:

- Chief Data Officers;
- Senior civil servants in Digital, Technology, Architecture, Information, Product and Service roles;
- Senior civil servants responsible for strategy within public sector organisations and departments who do not have data specific job titles;
- Third-party suppliers undertaking large scale data-focused initiatives on behalf of a public sector organisation.

## Principle writing conventions

The data principles are formulated in a way that mirrors other related principles, notably those set out in [The Open Group Architecture Framework](https://pubs.opengroup.org/architecture/togaf9-doc/arch/chap20.html). They are split into three parts:

- **Statement:** A singular sentence that summarises the principle.
- **Rationale:** Justification as to why this principle is important for the specification.
- **Implications:** Things you need to consider or do to practice this principle.

However, some principles are more novel than others; novel principles are not yet substantiated by established policy and procedures. In this document, the implications are RAG rated to approximate how easily the principle could be exercised across government under current conditions. The colours can be interpreted to signal:

- **Green** - clear implications; well provisioned; not much new work to exercise principle.
- **Amber** - complex implications; well provisioned; some new work to exercise principle.
- **Red** - complex implications; poorly provisioned; much new work to exercise principle.
